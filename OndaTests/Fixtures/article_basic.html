The Long Migration

By Jordan Reyes

Arctic terns make the longest migration of any animal on Earth, travelling from their Arctic breeding grounds to the Antarctic and back again every single year. Researchers who fitted the birds with tiny geolocators found round trips exceeding seventy thousand kilometres, a distance that comfortably eclipses earlier estimates and rewrites what biologists thought a four-ounce seabird could endure.

The birds do not fly in straight lines. Instead they trace enormous looping detours that follow prevailing wind systems over the Atlantic and Indian Oceans, trading distance for energy savings the way sailors once traded time for the trade winds. A tern that hugged the great-circle route would fight headwinds for weeks; the loop costs kilometres but spares the wing muscles that must last three decades of annual journeys.

What guides them remains only partly understood. Magnetic sensing, star patterns, polarized light and even smell have all been implicated in experiments, yet no single mechanism explains how a first-year bird with no map and no guide finds a specific stretch of Antarctic pack ice ten thousand kilometres from the nest where it hatched.

For the researchers, the harder question is what climate change does to a species whose life is stretched between the two fastest-warming regions on the planet. Earlier ice breakup shifts the krill blooms the terns depend on, and the long-term data sets needed to detect a decline are only now reaching maturity.
